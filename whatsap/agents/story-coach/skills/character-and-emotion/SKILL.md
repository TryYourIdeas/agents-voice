# Character and Emotion

The base coaching prompt scores "Character" (can we understand who mattered, what they wanted,
what they believed) and "Emotional Engagement" (does the audience understand what the storyteller
experienced) but doesn't give concrete techniques for either. This skill is that toolkit. Reach
for it when a story's people feel like names rather than people, or its emotion feels asserted
("I was nervous") rather than felt.

## The core principle: specificity creates empathy

Every technique below serves one goal: **abstraction is forgettable, specificity is felt.** A
listener can't emotionally connect to "it was a stressful time" — there's nothing concrete to
picture. They connect instantly to "my hands were shaking so hard I couldn't hold the pen." This
is the same mechanism as the Specificity dimension in the base prompt, applied specifically to
people and feelings rather than events.

**Coaching cue:** whenever a story names an emotion or a trait directly ("she was demanding," "I
was scared"), ask "what did that look like?" or "what did that feel like in your body?" The answer
is almost always more specific and more powerful than the abstraction it replaces — use the
answer, not the label, in the retelling.

## Diagnostic: is this a character/emotion problem?

Ask two questions about the story as told:
1. **Could a listener describe any person in this story in one concrete sentence** (what they
   wanted, one distinguishing trait or action)? If every person is just a name and a role
   ("my manager," "a coworker"), it's a character problem.
2. **Does the story ever describe a physical or behavioral sign of emotion**, rather than only
   naming the emotion ("I was stressed")? If emotion only ever appears as a label, it's an
   emotion problem.

If either is true, this skill is the highest-leverage fix — often more so than a structural fix,
because a listener who can't picture anyone or feel anything won't stay engaged long enough for
structure to matter.

## Techniques

### 1. Want vs. need

Every well-drawn character in a story has a **want** (the concrete, external thing they're
pursuing — get the promotion, hit the deadline, fix the outage) and often a deeper **need** (the
internal thing the story is really about — trust the team, ask for help, let go of control). The
want drives the plot; the gap between want and need is usually where the story's real meaning
lives.

**Coaching cue:** ask "what did you want in that moment?" first — get a concrete, external answer.
Then ask "looking back, was there something you actually needed that's different from that?" If
the user can name a need that's different from the want, that gap is usually the strongest
material for the Meaning/Impact dimension — often stronger than whatever "lesson" they'd otherwise
default to stating directly.

### 2. Physiology over labels (show, don't tell, for emotion)

Replace a named emotion with the physical sensation, involuntary behavior, or thought that
accompanied it. This is the single most reliable emotion fix.

- Weak: "I was really anxious before the meeting."
- Strong: "I read the same email four times and still couldn't tell you what it said."

**Coaching cue:** for any line where the user names a feeling, ask "what happened in your body, or
what did you do, right at that moment?" Take whatever concrete answer they give and have them swap
it in for the label. If they can't think of one, ask "what were you doing with your hands / where
were you looking / what were you thinking, word for word?" — almost everyone has a concrete answer
once asked directly, even if it didn't occur to them to include it unprompted.

### 3. The emotional beat (don't rush the felt moment)

When a story reaches its most emotional point, resist the urge to summarize it and move to the
next plot beat. Slow down for one extra sentence exactly there — what the moment felt like, not
just what happened next.

- Weak: "...and then I found out we'd lost the client, so I called an emergency meeting."
- Strong: "...and then I found out we'd lost the client. I remember just staring at the screen for
  a second before I could even move. Then I called an emergency meeting."

**Coaching cue:** this is a pacing fix specific to the emotional peak, distinct from the base
prompt's general Pacing dimension. Ask "what's the one sentence in this story where the most is at
stake?" then "did you slow down there, or rush through it?" Most first-tellings rush exactly the
moment that most deserves a beat.

### 4. Secondary character economy

Give any character who isn't the protagonist exactly one vivid, specific trait or action —
enough to make them a person, not so much that the listener has to work to track multiple named
people. This matters especially for spoken stories (no visual aid, nothing to reread), where too
many under-defined names is one of the most common causes of a listener losing the thread.

- Weak: naming four coworkers with no distinguishing detail between them.
- Strong: "My manager — the one who always ate lunch at his desk — was the only one who didn't
  seem worried."

**Coaching cue:** if a story names more than 2-3 people, check whether each one actually needs to
be named at all, or could be folded into "a coworker" / "the team." For any person who does need a
name, ask for one concrete detail (a habit, a line they said, a role) and cut everything else
about them.

### 5. Dialogue as character revelation

A short, real line of what someone said reveals more about who they are than a description of
them does — it shows the listener their voice, values, or state of mind directly, without the
storyteller interpreting it first.

- Weak: "My manager was really supportive and encouraging."
- Strong: "My manager just said, 'Fix what you can today, worry about the rest tomorrow.'"

**Coaching cue:** ask "did anyone say anything, word for word, that you remember?" — even a
half-remembered paraphrase is usually more vivid than a trait description, and it's a natural
companion to the Dialogue Open technique in the hook-and-opening-techniques skill.

## Choosing a technique

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Story names emotions directly ("I was stressed/excited/nervous") | Physiology over labels |
| Story's ending states a generic lesson that doesn't feel earned | Want vs. need — find the gap |
| Story rushes past its most emotional moment to the next event | The emotional beat |
| Listener can't keep track of who's who | Secondary character economy |
| A character is described only in traits, never shown acting/speaking | Dialogue as character revelation |

As with other skills, introduce at most one technique per round of feedback (Coaching Rule 1).
Physiology-over-labels is usually the highest-leverage single fix when in doubt — check for named
emotions first, before reaching for want/need or economy fixes.
