# Impact and Meaning

The base coaching prompt scores "Meaning / Impact" (why does this story matter, avoid generic
lessons that don't emerge naturally) but doesn't give concrete techniques for getting there. This
skill is that toolkit. Reach for it when a story's ending states a lesson that could be pasted onto
almost any story ("I learned the importance of teamwork") rather than one that could only have come
from these specific events.

## The core test: could this lesson be pasted onto a different story?

Read the story's stated takeaway on its own, without the story attached. If it's generic enough
that it could follow almost any story with a happy ending ("I learned resilience," "teamwork
matters," "never give up"), it hasn't been earned by *this* story specifically — it's a label, not
a meaning. A real, earned meaning references something only this story's specific events could
have produced.

**Coaching cue:** ask the user to say their takeaway line out loud, then ask "could you say that
exact sentence after a completely different story with a different problem?" If yes, that's the
signal to dig deeper with the techniques below rather than accept the line as-is.

## Diagnostic: is this a meaning problem?

A story can score well on every other dimension and still feel unsatisfying if the ending doesn't
land. Check: does the story's ending reference the *specific* obstacle, choice, or cost from
earlier in the story, or does it float free of the particulars — a moral that was stated rather
than demonstrated? If it floats free, this skill is the fix, even if structure, tension, and
character are all otherwise strong.

## Techniques

### 1. Show the change, don't state the lesson

Instead of naming an abstract lesson, describe a concrete behavior, decision, or perspective that's
different now because of the story — something the listener can picture, not just agree with.

- Stated (weak): "This experience taught me the importance of asking for help."
- Shown (strong): "Now, the second I feel stuck on something for more than an hour, I message
  someone. I didn't used to do that."

**Coaching cue:** for any "I learned that ___" line, ask "what do you actually do differently now,
specifically, because of that?" Swap the abstract lesson for the concrete behavior change — it's
almost always more convincing and more specific to this exact story.

### 2. The "so what?" chain

Starting from the surface-level outcome, ask "so what?" repeatedly, each time pushing past the
previous answer, until the answer stops being generic and starts being specific to this person's
actual experience. Three or four rounds usually gets past the first, most generic answer.

- Round 1: "We hit the deadline." → So what?
- Round 2: "So the client stayed." → So what?
- Round 3: "So I learned deadlines matter." *(still generic — keep going)* → So what does that mean
  for how you work now?
- Round 4: "So now I flag risk two weeks early instead of hoping it resolves itself, because I know
  exactly what it costs when it doesn't." *(specific, earned)*

**Coaching cue:** when a user's first-pass takeaway sounds generic, don't reject it outright — ask
"and so what does that mean for you, specifically?" one more time. The generic version is usually
just an intermediate stop, not a dead end.

### 3. Before/after contrast

Make the change tangible by briefly and explicitly contrasting who the storyteller was before the
story's events against who they are after — not just implying it, stating the contrast directly in
one compact line.

- "I used to think asking for help looked like weakness. Now I think it's the fastest way to not
  waste two more days being stuck."

**Coaching cue:** ask "what did you believe before this happened that you don't believe anymore
(or believe differently now)?" This is a direct, teachable prompt for generating a before/after
line when the user is struggling to articulate the meaning on their own.

### 4. The cost-plus-change test

An ending only feels earned if something was genuinely paid for it — real meaning tends to require
real cost (this is the same idea as the Story Circle's "Take" step in story-structure-frameworks,
applied specifically to whether the *ending* lands). A lesson attached to a story where nothing was
actually risked or lost reads as unearned, no matter how well it's phrased.

**Coaching cue:** ask "what did it cost you to get to this outcome?" If the answer is "nothing,
really, it just worked out" — that's worth naming directly: without a real cost, the ending may
need either a more honest, lower-key takeaway, or the story needs to surface a cost that's already
there but wasn't mentioned (a risk taken, a relationship strained, time or credibility spent).

### 5. Balance the particular and the universal

The strongest endings are specific enough to feel true to this one story, but land on something the
listener recognizes from their own life too. Too specific-only and the ending feels like it doesn't
generalize beyond this one anecdote; too universal-only (generic self-help phrasing) and it feels
unearned, disconnected from the specific events (see technique 1).

**Coaching cue:** after the user states a specific, concrete takeaway (via technique 1 or 2), ask
"is there a more universal version of that same idea, in your own words, that other people would
recognize from their own experience?" — the goal is a specific line that also resonates, not a
switch to generic phrasing.

## Choosing a technique

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Ending states an abstract lesson ("I learned X matters") | Show the change, don't state the lesson |
| User's first-pass takeaway is generic, feels like a placeholder | The "so what?" chain |
| Change is implied but never stated directly | Before/after contrast |
| Ending feels unearned even though it's well-phrased | Cost-plus-change test — check if anything was actually at stake |
| Takeaway is either too specific to generalize or too generic to feel true | Balance the particular and the universal |

As with other skills, introduce at most one technique per round of feedback (Coaching Rule 1). The
"so what?" chain and "show the change" are usually the highest-leverage starting points — check
those two before reaching for before/after contrast or the cost test.
