# Pacing, Clarity, and Authenticity

The base coaching prompt scores "Pacing" and "Language & Clarity" as dimensions, and Coaching Rule
2 says to preserve the user's voice rather than making them sound like an AI — but none of these
come with concrete techniques. This skill is that toolkit, covering all three together because
they interact: a story can become clearer and better-paced by *cutting*, but over-editing in that
same pass is exactly what erodes authenticity. Watch for that trade-off throughout.

## Pacing

### The core principle: narration time should track importance, not real-world duration

A five-minute conversation that changed everything deserves more sentences than a two-day wait
that didn't. Most first-tellings do the opposite by default — they narrate roughly in proportion
to how long things *actually took*, which buries the important moment under equally-weighted
background.

**Coaching cue:** ask "which single moment in this story mattered most?" Then check: does it get
proportionally more airtime than the setup and connective moments around it, or the same amount?
If the same, that's the fix — not by adding detail everywhere, but by cutting elsewhere (see
below) so the important moment stands out by contrast.

### Technique: cut connective tissue

Transitional filler between important moments ("so then I went home, and the next day I woke up,
and I got to the office, and...") should compress to almost nothing. Jump straight from one
important moment to the next; the listener doesn't need the connective travel narrated.

- Weak: "So I left the meeting, walked back to my desk, sat down, opened my laptop, and then I
  saw the email."
- Strong: "I got back to my desk and saw the email."

**Coaching cue:** listen for a string of three or more sequential minor actions (walked, sat,
opened) with no tension in any of them — that's connective tissue. Ask "do we need all of these
steps, or just the destination?"

### Technique: fast-forward summary + slow-motion scene

Explicitly summarize unimportant stretches of time in a single line, then slow all the way down
into a fully detailed scene for the moment that matters — the contrast between the two speeds is
itself what makes the important moment feel important.

- "For the next two weeks we just grinded — nothing dramatic, just work. Then, on a Thursday
  afternoon, everything changed."

**Coaching cue:** this pairs with "the emotional beat" technique in character-and-emotion — the
fast-forward creates the contrast that makes the subsequent slow-down land. If a story is uniformly
paced throughout (no fast parts, no slow parts), that flatness is often the actual pacing problem,
more than any individual section being too long.

### Technique: sentence length as rhythm

Short sentences read and sound faster — useful at tense or urgent moments. Longer, flowing
sentences slow the perceived pace — useful for reflective or emotional moments. Mismatching these
(long sentences during the tense peak, short choppy ones during a reflective moment) works against
the story's own pacing.

- Tense moment, matched: "The phone rang. I didn't answer. It rang again."
- Reflective moment, matched: "I sat there for a while, not really thinking about anything in
  particular, just letting it settle."

**Coaching cue:** at the story's tensest point, check sentence length. If it's still using long,
multi-clause sentences there, that's often why a technically-tense moment doesn't *feel* tense when
told aloud.

## Clarity

### Technique: one idea per sentence

Spoken stories are consumed once, in real time, with no rereading — a sentence carrying two or
three ideas at once is much harder to follow spoken than written. Split compound sentences at
natural breakpoints.

- Weak: "So basically what happened was my manager, who I'd only met once before because she'd
  just transferred from another team, told me in a meeting that included two other people that
  the project was being cancelled."
- Strong: "My manager had just transferred to our team — I'd only met her once. In a meeting with
  two other people, she told me the project was being cancelled."

**Coaching cue:** if a sentence needs a breath in the middle when said aloud, it's probably two
sentences. Ask the user to say the line out loud — natural breath points usually reveal where to
split it.

### Technique: concrete referents

Avoid pronouns that could point to more than one person or thing already mentioned. Once two
people are in a story, "he," "she," or "they" needs to stay unambiguous, or be replaced with a
name/role.

- Weak: "I told my manager that he was wrong, and he said that he'd think about it." (Two "he"s —
  ambiguous which one thinks about it.)
- Strong: "I told my manager he was wrong. He said he'd think about it." — same issue, still
  ambiguous when spoken; needs "My manager said *he'd* think about it" or similar disambiguation.

**Coaching cue:** whenever two people of the same gender (or two things) are both active in a
sentence, check every pronoun after the first mention — read it back and ask "would a stranger
know who this refers to?"

### Technique: cut or define unexplained jargon

Any acronym, internal term, or unexplained reference forces the listener to either silently ask
"what's that?" (losing the next few seconds of the story) or give up trying to picture it. Define
in three words inline, or cut the term and describe the thing plainly instead.

- Weak: "We were behind on the Q3 OKRs because the migration to the new CRM slipped."
- Strong: "We were behind on our quarterly goals because a system migration slipped."

**Coaching cue:** ask "would someone outside your company/field understand every term in this
story?" For interview-context stories especially, unexplained internal jargon is one of the most
common, most fixable clarity problems.

## Authenticity

### The core principle: authentic ≠ polished

A story edited toward generic, "impressive-sounding" phrasing often becomes *less* convincing, not
more — audiences trust specific, slightly imperfect, recognizably-personal accounts more than
suspiciously smooth ones. Coaching Rule 2 already says "don't turn the user's story into polished
corporate language" — these are the concrete moves that preserve or restore that.

### Technique: keep the user's actual words

When helping tighten a sentence, prefer trimming the user's own phrasing over substituting
"better-sounding" vocabulary. If they said "it was a mess," don't upgrade that to "it was
chaotic and unsustainable" unless that's genuinely closer to what they meant — ask, don't assume.

**Coaching cue:** after suggesting a rewrite, ask "does that still sound like something you'd
actually say?" If the user hesitates or says it feels off, that's a signal the edit drifted toward
generic polish rather than the user's own voice — revert toward their original wording.

### Technique: imperfection as a trust signal

A small detail that doesn't flatter the teller — a mistake, a moment of doubt, an admission of not
knowing what to do — usually increases how believable and relatable a story feels, not decreases
it. A story that presents the teller as flawless in every moment reads as less credible, not more
impressive.

- Less trusted: "I immediately knew exactly what to do and calmly fixed it."
- More trusted: "For about ten seconds I had no idea what to do. Then I just started with the
  first thing I could think of."

**Coaching cue:** if a story presents the user as having handled everything perfectly throughout,
ask "was there a moment you doubted yourself, or almost did the wrong thing?" That moment, once
found, is usually worth including even though it's less flattering — it's specifically what makes
the eventual outcome feel earned rather than assumed.

### Technique: match register to context, don't flatten it

Authenticity isn't the same as always being casual — a job-interview story can be authentic while
still being more composed than a story told to close friends. The target is "genuinely the user,
in this context," not one fixed style regardless of audience.

**Coaching cue:** ask what the story is for (per the base prompt's context-setting) before
suggesting tone changes — a suggestion that's right for a casual retelling may be wrong for an
interview-prep version of the same story, and vice versa.

## Choosing a technique

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Story spends equal time on important and unimportant moments | Time-tracks-importance — find the moment that matters, cut around it |
| String of minor sequential actions with no tension | Cut connective tissue |
| Story feels uniformly paced, nothing stands out | Fast-forward summary + slow-motion scene |
| Tense moment told in long, unhurried sentences | Sentence length as rhythm |
| A sentence needs a breath in the middle when spoken | One idea per sentence |
| Pronoun could refer to more than one person/thing | Concrete referents |
| Acronym or internal term a stranger wouldn't know | Cut or define jargon |
| Retelling sounds polished but doesn't sound like the user anymore | Keep the user's actual words |
| Story presents the user as flawless throughout | Imperfection as a trust signal |

As with other skills, introduce at most one technique per round of feedback (Coaching Rule 1). When
a pacing or clarity fix and an authenticity concern pull in different directions — tightening a
sentence versus keeping the user's own phrasing — authenticity wins; a slightly looser sentence
that still sounds like the user beats a tighter one that doesn't.
