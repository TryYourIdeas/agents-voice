# Tension and Conflict

The base coaching prompt scores "Conflict / Tension" (obstacles, uncertainty, risk, competing
goals, consequences) but doesn't give concrete techniques for building it. This skill is that
toolkit. Reach for it when a story technically has an obstacle but the listener never feels
uncertain about the outcome, or when obstacles just repeat rather than escalate.

## The core test: so what if it fails?

Every technique below serves one question: **can the listener answer "what happens if this
doesn't work out?"** If a story never establishes what's actually at stake, no amount of incident
will feel tense, because there's nothing to lose. If the listener can answer that question clearly
and the answer matters, almost any obstacle will carry some tension automatically.

**Coaching cue:** ask the user directly, "what would have happened if this had gone wrong?" If they
have a clear, specific answer, check whether that answer is actually in the story as told — stakes
that exist only in the teller's head don't create tension for the listener. If they don't have a
clear answer, that's the real problem, ahead of any technique below — find or clarify the stakes
first.

## Diagnostic: is this a tension problem?

Ask: **does the story include a moment where the outcome is genuinely uncertain** — where a
listener hearing it for the first time wouldn't know how it turns out? If every event in the story
resolves immediately and cleanly as it's mentioned, tension is very likely the highest-leverage
fix, even if the events themselves are interesting.

## Techniques

### 1. Escalation / try-fail cycles

A character's attempts to solve the problem should get progressively harder, not repeat at the
same difficulty. Each attempt should either fail and make things worse, or partially succeed while
creating a new complication (borrowed from the improv principle "yes, but / no, and" — never a
clean "yes, and" that just resolves the problem outright mid-story).

- Weak: "I tried reaching out to the client. Then I tried again the next day. Then I finally got
  through."
- Strong: "I called the client — straight to voicemail. I emailed — no reply. By the third day I
  was drafting the message we'd send if we lost the account entirely."

**Coaching cue:** count the obstacles in the story. If there are two or more, check whether attempt
#2 is actually harder or different from attempt #1, or just a repeat. Ask "what made the second
attempt worse than the first?" — if there's no answer, the story needs either a real escalation or
should cut the repeated beat entirely.

### 2. The ticking clock

An explicit or implied time constraint raises tension even on otherwise mundane stakes, because it
removes the option of waiting the problem out.

- Weak: "We needed to fix the bug at some point before the client noticed."
- Strong: "We had four hours before the client's demo, and the bug only showed up under load."

**Coaching cue:** ask "was there a deadline, explicit or unspoken?" Almost every real story has one
somewhere (a meeting time, a flight, a shift ending) even if the user didn't think to mention it —
surface it and put it early enough that the listener is tracking it during the tense moments, not
told about it only after the fact.

### 3. Withholding resolution

Once the story reaches its point of maximum uncertainty, resist resolving it in the very next
clause. Let the uncertainty sit for a beat before revealing the outcome — this is the tension
equivalent of the emotional beat technique (character-and-emotion skill), applied to plot instead
of feeling.

- Weak: "I hit send on the email, and it turned out fine — they loved it."
- Strong: "I hit send. And then there was nothing — for two days, nothing. I checked my inbox more
  times than I'd like to admit. When the reply finally came, it just said: 'Can we talk?'"

**Coaching cue:** find the single sentence in the story where the outcome is revealed. Ask "what's
the version of this where I don't find out right away?" — even one extra sentence of unresolved
uncertainty before the reveal usually raises the Conflict/Tension score more than any added
incident would.

### 4. Dramatic irony vs. mystery — control what the listener knows

Two different ways to create suspense, and they're opposites:
- **Mystery**: the listener knows only what you knew at the time, and discovers things as you did.
  Creates "what happens next?" tension.
- **Dramatic irony**: the listener is told up front something you didn't know at the time (e.g.
  "I had no idea this was about to go completely sideways"), creating "how does this go wrong?"
  tension instead.

Both work; mixing them unintentionally (accidentally revealing the outcome early, then trying to
build suspense about it anyway) undercuts tension.

**Coaching cue:** ask the user which one they're doing, on purpose. If they've been telling the
story in mystery mode but let slip the ending early ("...and that's how I ended up getting
promoted, but let me back up"), either commit to dramatic irony deliberately (lean into it — use
the flash-forward hook technique) or cut the early reveal and stay in mystery mode consistently.

### 5. Competing goals (the internal dilemma)

The strongest tension often isn't a character against an external obstacle at all — it's a
character wanting two incompatible things at once, forcing a real choice. External obstacles
create suspense; competing internal goals create a dilemma, which tends to be the more memorable
and more personally revealing kind of tension (and pairs directly with the want-vs-need technique
in character-and-emotion).

- External obstacle only: "The deadline was tight and the client kept changing requirements."
- Competing goals: "I could hit the deadline by cutting corners, or protect the team's trust by
  telling the client we needed more time — I couldn't do both."

**Coaching cue:** ask "was there a version of this where you had to choose between two things you
both wanted?" If yes, that choice — not just the external obstacle — is usually the real core of
the story's tension, and often deserves more airtime than the external pressure that forced it.

## Choosing a technique

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| No clear answer to "what happens if this fails?" | Fix stakes clarity first — before any technique below |
| Multiple obstacles that all feel the same difficulty | Escalation / try-fail cycles |
| Story has stakes but they don't feel urgent | The ticking clock |
| Every complication resolves in the very next sentence | Withholding resolution |
| Outcome got revealed too early, undercutting suspense | Dramatic irony vs. mystery — pick one on purpose |
| Story is externally tense but doesn't feel personal | Competing goals — look for the internal dilemma |

As with other skills, introduce at most one technique per round of feedback (Coaching Rule 1).
Stakes clarity is the prerequisite for all the others — if the listener can't answer "so what if it
fails," fix that before reaching for escalation, clocks, or withholding.
