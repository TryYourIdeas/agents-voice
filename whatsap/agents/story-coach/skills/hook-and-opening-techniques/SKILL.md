# Hook and Opening Techniques

The base coaching prompt scores "Hook" (does the opening create curiosity, tension, surprise, or
emotional interest) but doesn't give concrete openings to retell against. This skill is that
toolkit. Reach for it whenever a story's *first sentence* is the problem — even if everything
after it is strong — because a weak opening is the single highest-leverage fix available: in a
spoken story, a bored listener in the first five seconds rarely re-engages no matter how good the
ending is.

## Why hooks work: the curiosity gap

Every technique below is really one underlying mechanism: open a **gap between what the listener
knows and what they want to know**, then delay closing it. Psychologist George Loewenstein's
information-gap theory is the underlying reason a great first line pulls people in — curiosity is
what humans feel when they notice a gap in their own knowledge. A weak opening supplies
information the listener didn't ask for yet (background, setup, logistics); a strong opening
withholds exactly the piece of information the listener now *wants*.

**Coaching cue:** for any opening the user tries, ask "what question does this line make me want
answered?" If you can't name one, there's no gap yet — it's not a hook, it's an announcement.

## Diagnostic: is this a hook problem?

Time (or count sentences) how long it takes the story to reach its first real point of tension,
surprise, or stakes. If it's more than ~2 sentences or ~10 seconds of spoken time before something
is at stake or in question, the opening is very likely the highest-impact fix — ahead of anything
about the middle or end of the story.

## Techniques

### 1. In medias res (start in the middle of the action)

Drop the listener into the most tense or active moment of the story, mid-scene, before any
context. Backfill the "how did I get here" context afterward, in a sentence or two, once curiosity
is already established.

- Weak: "So this was back when I was working at Bicevida, and the team situation was pretty
  difficult at the time..."
- Strong: "I opened my laptop on a Monday morning and every single developer had quit."

**Coaching cue:** ask the user "what's the single most tense or interesting moment in this story?"
Then: "Start there. Tell me the rest of the setup afterward, in one sentence, only if I need it."

### 2. The question hook

Open with a direct or implied question the rest of the story answers. Works especially well for
interview-context stories, since it frames the story as answering something the listener (the
interviewer) cares about.

- "What do you do when the entire team you're supposed to lead has already quit?"

**Coaching cue:** works best paired with in medias res, not alone — a question with no scene behind
it can feel like a rhetorical trick rather than a real hook.

### 3. Flash-forward / stakes preview

State a striking outcome or consequence up front, then rewind to tell how it happened. Useful when
the story's ending is its strongest asset and the middle is comparatively plain.

- "By the end of that year, we'd rebuilt the entire team from scratch — but it almost didn't
  happen, because of one meeting in March."

**Coaching cue:** if the user's story has a great ending but a flat opening, this is usually the
right fix — borrow a piece of the ending and move it to the front.

### 4. Dialogue open

Open with a line someone actually said — verbatim if possible. Spoken dialogue drops the listener
directly into a scene without narration, and a striking or unexpected line does double duty as a
hook.

- "'We're not launching Monday.' That's the first thing my manager said to me."

**Coaching cue:** only works if the line itself is genuinely striking or surprising. A flat line of
dialogue ("She said hello") is not a hook — check that the quoted line itself creates a gap.

### 5. The confession / vulnerability hook

Open by naming the fear, doubt, or flaw the story is really about, stated plainly and without
hedging. Works well for stories about failure or growth (interview-prep contexts especially).

- "I almost quit two weeks into that job."

**Coaching cue:** this only works if it's genuinely true and specific — a vague vulnerability
("I struggled a lot") doesn't create a gap because there's nothing concrete to wonder about yet.

### 6. The wrong-assumption hook

Open by stating what you (or someone in the story) believed at the time, setting up a later
reversal. Creates a gap because the listener now wants to know how/whether that belief held up.

- "I was sure I'd be fired by Friday."

**Coaching cue:** pair this with Coaching Rule 4 from the base prompt (distinguish events from
narrative) — this technique only works if the belief is later actually tested or overturned in the
story, not just stated and forgotten.

## What to cut: throat-clearing patterns

These are the most common opening failures — recognizable enough to name directly when coaching:

- **Chronological wind-up:** "So basically what happened was..." / "So this was a while back..." —
  delays the hook by narrating that a story is about to start, instead of just starting it.
- **Logistics-first:** opening with when/where/who's-involved before anything is at stake. A little
  scene-setting can follow the hook; it should almost never precede it.
- **Apologizing or hedging:** "This might not be that interesting, but..." — actively lowers
  the listener's expectations before the hook has a chance to raise them. Always cut.
- **Telegraphing the theme:** "This story taught me a lot about resilience" as an opening line —
  this is the *ending's* insight, stated too early; it closes the curiosity gap before it opens,
  since the listener now knows the lesson and has less reason to wonder how it's earned.

**Coaching cue:** when a user's opening has one of these patterns, name the pattern directly
("that's a chronological wind-up") rather than only saying "make it punchier" — this is a more
teachable, recognizable unit than generic delivery advice, and the user can learn to self-diagnose
it in future stories.

## Choosing a technique

| Situation | Reach for |
|---|---|
| Story has a plain start but a great middle/end | Flash-forward — borrow from the ending |
| Story has a striking moment buried in the middle | In medias res — open there instead |
| Someone said something notable early in the real events | Dialogue open |
| Story is for an interview / needs to frame around a question | Question hook |
| Story is about failure, doubt, or growth | Confession or wrong-assumption hook |
| Opening currently has throat-clearing/logistics/hedging | Cut it — don't add a technique, subtract the wind-up first |

As with other skills, introduce at most one technique per round of feedback (Coaching Rule 1).
Fixing throat-clearing by cutting it is usually higher-leverage than adding a new technique on top
of an already-slow opening — check for that first.
