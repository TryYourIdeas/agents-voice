# Argumentation Strategies

These are rhetorical/persuasion techniques, not reasoning errors — a text
can use one of these and still be logically sound, or use one alongside a
genuine fallacy. Keep this distinction explicit in a report: naming a
strategy here is not the same claim as naming a fallacy from
`logical-fallacies`.

## Anchoring
Leading with an extreme number or claim to shift the perceived range of
what's reasonable, even if the anchor itself is never defended.
*Example:* opening a price negotiation with an absurdly high number so the
eventual "compromise" still favors the anchorer.

## Framing
Presenting the same facts through a particular lens (loss vs. gain,
threat vs. opportunity) that shapes the conclusion the audience reaches,
without changing the underlying facts.
*Example:* "90% survival rate" vs. "10% mortality rate" for the same
statistic.

## Gish Gallop
Overwhelming a listener/reader with a rapid volume of individually weak
arguments or claims, such that responding to all of them is impractical and
the sheer quantity reads as strength.
*Example:* a list of twenty loosely-related claims in one breath, each
too minor to fact-check individually before the conversation moves on.

## Steelmanning vs. Strawmanning
Steelmanning: presenting the opposing view in its strongest, most
charitable form before responding to it. Strawmanning: the reverse (see
`logical-fallacies`' Straw Man entry) — this pairing exists here because
noting *which* one a text does is itself a useful observation about its
argumentative honesty, independent of whether any specific fallacy occurred.

## Overton-Window Shifting
Introducing a more extreme position specifically to make a previously
fringe position look moderate by comparison.
*Example:* proposing an extreme policy first so a merely aggressive one
seems like the reasonable middle ground.

## Selective Emphasis / Cherry-Picking
Choosing which true facts to highlight (and which to omit) to steer a
conclusion, without stating anything false. Distinct from the Texas
Sharpshooter *fallacy* (see `logical-fallacies`) — cherry-picking is the
technique of selective presentation; Texas Sharpshooter is specifically the
fallacy of claiming a *pattern* exists because of the cherry-picked
selection.

## False Balance
Presenting two sides as equally credible/weighted when the actual evidence
strongly favors one, in the name of "fairness."
*Example:* giving a fringe claim and a well-evidenced consensus equal
airtime as though they're symmetric.

## Concern Trolling
Framing criticism or opposition as good-faith concern for the target's own
interests, to seem reasonable while actually undermining the position.
*Example:* "I just worry this policy will actually hurt the people it's
meant to help" from someone who opposes the policy's goals entirely.

## Legitimate Socratic Questioning
Asking a sequence of genuine questions to guide someone toward examining
their own reasoning — included here specifically as a *non-manipulative*
technique, to contrast with loaded questions (see `logical-fallacies`) and
concern trolling above. The test: are the questions asked in good faith,
open to any answer, versus steering toward a predetermined "gotcha"?
