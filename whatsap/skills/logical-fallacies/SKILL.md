# Logical Fallacies

Reference taxonomy. Consult this when you've spotted something that might be
a fallacy — don't name one unless it actually matches an entry below. If
nothing here fits what you're seeing, say "none identified" rather than
inventing a name.

For each fallacy: what it is, an example, and how to distinguish it from a
similar but valid argument — the distinguishing test is the important part,
since most of these are easy to over-apply to arguments that only look
similar on the surface.

## Straw Man
Misrepresenting an opponent's argument in a weaker/more extreme form, then
attacking that instead of the real position.
*Example:* "You want stricter emissions rules? So you want to ban all cars?"
*Not a fallacy when:* the restated version is a fair, uncharitable-but-
accurate paraphrase the arguer would actually endorse (a strong version of
their claim, even if you disagree with it) — that's just disagreement, not
misrepresentation.

## False Dichotomy (False Dilemma)
Presenting only two options as if they're the only possibilities, when more
exist.
*Example:* "Either we cut the budget entirely or the program fails."
*Not a fallacy when:* the situation genuinely only has two live options
(a true binary choice) — check whether a third option is actually
realistic before calling this out.

## Ad Hominem
Attacking the person making an argument instead of the argument itself.
*Example:* "Of course she says the plan is safe, she's not even an
engineer."
*Not a fallacy when:* the person's credibility is the actual subject under
discussion (e.g. assessing whether a witness is trustworthy) rather than
being used to dodge engaging with their argument's substance.

## Appeal to Authority
Treating a claim as true mainly because an authority figure said so, without
the authority's expertise actually being relevant or being used as a
substitute for evidence.
*Example:* "This diet works — a famous actor recommends it."
*Not a fallacy when:* the person cited has genuine, relevant expertise and
their statement is offered as *supporting* evidence among other evidence,
not as the sole proof. Citing a subject-matter expert on their subject is
normal, valid reasoning.

## Slippery Slope
Claiming one step will inevitably lead to a chain of increasingly extreme
consequences, without justifying that the chain is actually likely.
*Example:* "If we allow this exception, soon there'll be no rules at all."
*Not a fallacy when:* the chain of causation is actually demonstrated with
evidence for each link, not just asserted.

## Circular Reasoning (Begging the Question)
The conclusion is restated as one of the premises, so the argument assumes
what it's trying to prove.
*Example:* "The Bible is true because it says so, and it's true because
it's the word of God."
*Not a fallacy when:* a premise merely overlaps in *topic* with the
conclusion without actually presupposing it — the test is whether removing
the conclusion collapses the argument's logical support to nothing.

## Hasty Generalization
Drawing a broad conclusion from a sample too small or unrepresentative to
support it.
*Example:* "I met two rude people from that city, so everyone there is
rude."
*Not a fallacy when:* the sample size and diversity are actually adequate
for the claim being made — this is a matter of degree, not a bright line.

## False Cause (Post Hoc / Correlation-Causation)
Assuming that because B followed A, A caused B — or that correlation alone
proves causation.
*Example:* "Ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer, so ice
cream causes drowning."
*Not a fallacy when:* causation is independently established (mechanism,
controlled study, ruled-out confounders), not just inferred from
correlation or sequence.

## Red Herring
Introducing an irrelevant point to divert attention from the actual issue.
*Example:* "Why worry about the budget deficit when crime is the real
problem?"
*Not a fallacy when:* the new point is actually relevant context the
discussion needs, not a deflection.

## Motte-and-Bailey
Defending an easy-to-defend, modest claim (the "motte") while actually
asserting a more controversial one (the "bailey"), retreating to the modest
one whenever challenged.
*Example:* Arguing "traditional values matter" (modest) while actually
pushing a specific, contested policy, and retreating to the vague version
when pressed.
*Not a fallacy when:* someone genuinely just holds the modest claim and
isn't equivocating between the two.

## No True Scotsman
Redefining a category to exclude counterexamples after the fact, to protect
a generalization from being falsified.
*Example:* "No true fan would ever criticize the team." "But I'm a fan and
I criticize them." "Well then you're not a *true* fan."
*Not a fallacy when:* the definitional boundary was set in advance, before
the counterexample appeared, rather than adjusted reactively to dodge it.

## Equivocation
Using a word with more than one meaning in different parts of an argument as
though it means the same thing throughout.
*Example:* "The sign said 'fine for parking here,' so parking here must be
fine."
*Not a fallacy when:* the word is used consistently — check whether the
argument's logic actually depends on the meaning shifting mid-argument.

## Appeal to Emotion
Substituting an emotional appeal for actual evidence or reasoning.
*Example:* "Think of the children — you must support this law."
*Not a fallacy when:* the emotional content is accurate context supporting
an argument that also has independent evidence, not a replacement for it.

## Genetic Fallacy
Judging a claim's truth based on its origin/source rather than its actual
merits.
*Example:* "That idea came from a biased think tank, so it must be wrong."
*Not a fallacy when:* the source's bias is directly relevant to assessing
*reliability* of a factual claim (e.g. an unverified statistic from an
interested party) rather than being used to dismiss a claim's substance
outright without checking it.

## Tu Quoque ("You Too")
Deflecting criticism by pointing out the critic is guilty of the same
thing, instead of addressing the criticism.
*Example:* "You say I shouldn't smoke, but you used to smoke too."
*Not a fallacy when:* the inconsistency itself is the actual point under
discussion (e.g. assessing someone's credibility as a messenger), not used
to dodge the substance of the criticism.

## Sunk Cost Fallacy
Continuing a course of action because of resources already invested, rather
than the actual merits of continuing.
*Example:* "We've spent two years on this project, we can't stop now."
*Not a fallacy when:* the past investment created ongoing option value or
switching costs that are genuinely relevant to a forward-looking decision
(not just "we already spent money").

## Texas Sharpshooter
Cherry-picking data clusters to fit a pattern after the fact, ignoring data
that doesn't fit.
*Example:* Finding a "cancer cluster" by drawing the boundary around
wherever cases happened to occur, after the fact.
*Not a fallacy when:* the pattern was predicted in advance and then
confirmed, rather than found by searching after the fact for whatever
pattern exists.

## Burden of Proof Shifting
Demanding the other side disprove a claim rather than the claimant
supporting it.
*Example:* "Prove that this supplement doesn't work."
*Not a fallacy when:* both sides have already presented some evidence and
the discussion has moved to a genuinely shared evidentiary burden.

## Appeal to Ignorance
Claiming something is true because it hasn't been proven false (or vice
versa).
*Example:* "No one's proven ghosts don't exist, so they must be real."
*Not a fallacy when:* absence of evidence is being used cautiously as weak
evidence of absence in a domain where evidence would be expected if the
claim were true (not as proof either way).

## Loaded Question
Asking a question that contains a built-in assumption the respondent hasn't
agreed to.
*Example:* "Have you stopped mismanaging the project yet?"
*Not a fallacy when:* the embedded premise has already been established
and agreed upon earlier in the conversation.
